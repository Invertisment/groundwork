SAGA / TRANSACTION COMPOSITION

CROSS-CUTTING NOTE:
- Not a layer. A saga's decisions are core (see FUNCTIONAL_CORE.txt):
  a pure decider over the saga's recorded state. Around it are a
  generic runner in glue and the infra that stores the state and
  performs the steps. It adds no import rule of its own, so it gets no
  directory (see BOUNDARIES.txt ADDING A LAYER).

PROPERTY TEST:
- A saga exists exactly when a crash between steps would lose or
  orphan something. Its job is to make that loss visible and
  recoverable.
- Coordinates two or more independently-owned mutable resources within
  one logical operation, where no single atomic transaction can span
  all of them.
- Every decision (next step, retry, compensate, done) is a pure
  function of the saga's recorded state, and that state is durably
  saved before any step it decides runs.

WHY:
- Sequencing and retry-vs-compensate decisions are real business
  logic, not "just glue." Sagas break in practice not in the
  individual steps, but in orchestration someone assumed didn't need
  the same rigor as the core.
- As a pure decider, the orchestration gets that rigor: a failed step
  is just an input, so every failure combination can be generated and
  tested with plain data and no fakes. What can't be tested that way
  is the runner, and it's generic: written and tested once per
  project, not once per saga.

MAY NOT EXIST:
- Many operations are single-resource — no saga needed at all.
- Some cross-resource operations are better solved by reconciliation
  (an idempotent periodic sync) than by compensation, when the derived
  state can tolerate brief staleness (e.g. a local cache of an
  externally-owned flag).
- Check "does this touch more than one independently-owned resource,
  and is staleness actually unacceptable" before reaching for saga
  machinery. If either answer is no, there's no saga — see the
  single-writer / single-resource case (e.g. a game loop with one
  piece of in-memory state, or the QR scanner app with no DB).

DISTINGUISHING FROM A PLAIN CONTROLLER:
- A saga records durable progress — what step it's on — so a crash
  leaves a trail, not a silent void.
- A controller that just calls two things in sequence, with no durable
  record of intent, is NOT a saga even if it happens to touch two
  resources. It will produce orphaned/half-done state the first time
  the process dies mid-operation, with nothing for a human or an agent
  to diagnose from.

TWO KINDS:
- Coordinated (orchestration): one saga record and one decider own the
  whole operation and issue every step. The default: the operation's
  progress is in one place, which is what makes a crash diagnosable.
- Uncoordinated (choreography): no central record. Each participant
  has its own decider: an event in, its local state and outgoing
  events out. Progress is spread across participants, linked by a
  correlation id, so the trail exists per participant only. Chosen
  when the participants can't share an orchestrator (separate owners,
  separate deployments), and recorded in GROUNDWORK.md.

THE DECIDER (core):
- decide(state, result) -> (new state, commands), or
  next_action(record) -> command | done when the runner writes each
  result into the record itself. The spelling is per language, as for
  transitions (see GLUE.txt TRANSITIONS ARE NOT THE SAME THING AS
  "REDUCER").
- Commands and results are closed sets. Results mirror each infra
  operation's declared failure modes (see INFRA_LAYER.txt CONTRACT
  REQUIREMENT): a timeout and a rejection are different results, and
  the decider handles each.
- A step whose outcome is unknown (timed out, interrupted mid-call) is
  a state of its own, neither success nor failure: the decider retries
  it, since it's idempotent, or asks for its status.
- Belongs here: do/compensate pairs per step; retry-vs-rollback
  decisions (prefer retrying an idempotent pending step over rolling
  back an already-succeeded one, when possible); ordering
  preconditions between steps.
- The pivot is the step past which the saga only moves forward. Every
  step before it has a compensation; from it on the decider only
  retries forward. A step with no compensation can only be the pivot
  or come after it.
- In choreography, each participant's decider follows the same rules
  over its own state.

STATE STORAGE (infra, the project's choice):
- State-stored, the default: one durable record per saga (its id, each
  step's status, whether the pivot is passed). Simplest to query
  ("every saga stuck at payment").
- Event-sourced: results are stored as events and the state is a fold
  over them, evolve(state, event) -> state, in core next to the
  decider. Same pure functions as state-stored. Chosen only when the
  history itself is a requirement (audit, replaying the business
  process), since it costs event schema versioning, snapshots for long
  histories and harder queries; recorded in GROUNDWORK.md with that
  reason.
- A durable workflow engine (e.g. Temporal, Restate, AWS Step
  Functions): the saga is written as sequential code that the engine
  replays after a crash, which requires the workflow body to be
  deterministic — core's purity rule. The body is the decider, its
  activities are infra, and the engine is the runner, replacing the
  hand-written one (see REUSE.txt: a library before the project's own
  harness).
- Whichever it is, the saved state's format is a contract a later run
  depends on (recovery after a crash), so a test holds it: write it,
  read it back, resume.

THE RUNNER (glue):
- One generic loop, no domain branching: load the saga state, call the
  decider, save the new state with its commands marked pending, then
  execute the commands through infra and feed each result back in.
  Mapping a command to its infra call is a dispatch, not a decision.
- Save before execute. A crash after a call but before its result is
  saved runs that step again on resume, so every step and every
  compensation is idempotent (see INFRA_LAYER.txt CONTRACT
  REQUIREMENT).
- In choreography, a participant's state change and its outgoing
  events are saved in one transaction in its own store (a
  transactional outbox, infra), and a relay publishes them afterwards.
  The relay can publish an event twice, so consumers deduplicate by
  event id.

COMMENTS:
- The decider is core, so core's comment rules hold (see
  FUNCTIONAL_CORE.txt COMMENTS). The steps, their compensations and
  the record are code and tests, not prose; its one sentence says why
  the steps run in this order when nothing in the data forces it.

TESTING:
- The decider is tested like any core: plain data, no fakes.
  Property-based tests are the strongest fit. Generate the failing
  step, timeouts versus rejections, duplicated results and restarts
  (resuming from state saved mid-saga), and assert invariants hold
  regardless: nothing is left reserved or authorized once the saga
  ends, nothing is compensated after the pivot, every step completed
  before a failure is compensated exactly once, and replaying a result
  changes nothing. The failure space is combinatorial and easy to
  under-enumerate by hand; a generator covers combinations a human
  wouldn't write down. See DECOMPOSITION.txt for why this also guards
  against special-casing one failure sequence.
- Choreography: wire the participants' deciders together in memory,
  generate deliveries reordered, duplicated and delayed, and assert the
  same invariants over their combined state.
- The runner: a crash-injection test, once per runner, not per saga.
  Stop it at each point (before saving, after saving but before the
  call, after the call but before its result is saved), resume, and
  assert every step ran and the saved state matches. It runs
  synthetic commands, not the project's sagas. With a workflow engine,
  the engine's guarantees stand in for it.
- Saga logic needs no infra fakes. Where an end-to-end test runs a
  saga against fakes, they're contract-verified (see INFRA_LAYER.txt
  TESTING).
